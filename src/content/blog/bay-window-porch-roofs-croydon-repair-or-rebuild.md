Ask anyone in Croydon where their roof leaks and most will point at the main slope. In our experience a fair share of the calls we get about stains near the front of the house turn out to be the bay or the porch instead. These are the smallest roofs on the building and the least looked after. Croydon has thousands of them. The borough's Victorian and Edwardian terraces in Thornton Heath, Norbury and South Norwood were built with bay fronts as standard, and the interwar semis that cover Shirley, Selsdon and Coulsdon nearly all have one, plenty of them now about ninety years old. A bay window roof is often less than two square metres of lead, tile or felt, yet it sits right above a window and right against a wall, which is the worst place for a leak to start. This guide covers bay window and porch roofs on Croydon houses: how each type is built, why they fail, when to repair and when to rebuild, the council rules, and what the work costs in 2026.

## Bay window and porch roofs in Croydon: which kind have you got?

Across the borough there are four main types, and each one goes wrong differently. Knowing yours makes a roofer's phone diagnosis far more useful and stops you paying for the wrong fix.

<img src="/images/roof-repairs-croydon-loc-west-wickham-1930s-semi-tile-hung-bay-gable-w1200.webp" alt="Black and white view over a row of interwar semi-detached houses with two-storey bay windows under tiled roofs" width="972" height="648" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

The first is the single-storey bay on a Victorian terrace: a canted bay at ground floor with a small hipped roof of slate, sometimes slate-hung on its sloping faces, tucked under the first-floor window. The second is the two-storey Edwardian bay, which rises the full height of the front and finishes either in its own little gable or in a flat top covered in lead. The third is the 1930s version, where a tiled hood or a run of tiled roof carries across the top of the ground-floor bay and often keeps going over the front door as a porch canopy. The fourth is the flat-topped porch, added at almost any date since, usually covered in felt. If water is showing up at the front of the house and you aren't sure which roof it's coming from, our [roof leak repairs in Croydon](/roof-leak-repairs) page explains how we trace it before anything gets quoted.

## Victorian and Edwardian bays: lead tops, slate faces and hidden timber

The older bays are clever little structures, and the cleverness is the problem. On a two-storey Edwardian bay the brickwork of the upper floor often doesn't sit on brick at all. It sits on a timber head, sometimes called a bressummer, running across the top of the ground-floor windows.

<img src="/images/blog/south-london-victorian-terraces-bay-windows-city-view.webp" alt="Rows of Victorian brick terraces with bay windows running down a south London hill, with the City's towers in the distance" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

The roof of the bay protects that timber. A lead flat on a bay top was laid on boards over small joists, with a drip or two to let the lead move, and turned up against the main wall under a cover flashing. Slate-hung bay roofs on the Victorian terraces work the same way, just at a steeper pitch: small slates nailed to boards, with a lead flashing or a mortar fillet where they meet the house.

When either covering lets water through, it doesn't drip into a room straight away. It soaks into the boards, then the joists, then the head timber over the window. That can take years. Nobody notices. By the time a stain appears inside, the wood behind it has often been wet for several winters. On the Victorian and Edwardian streets of South Norwood and Upper Norwood, where many original bays survive, this is the most common hidden fault we come across on the front of the house.

## 1930s semis: tiled bay hoods and the flat-topped porch

The interwar builders who laid out Selsdon, Sanderstead and the roads off the Purley Way worked fast and to a price, and the bay is where it shows. The two-storey bay itself usually sits under a projection of the main hipped roof, which is sound enough. The weak spot is lower down: the small pent roof of plain tiles that runs across the front at first-floor level, over the ground-floor bay and the recessed porch.

<img src="/images/blog/period-bay-window-leaded-lights-window-seat-croydon.webp" alt="Inside a period bay window with leaded-light glazing, panelled walls below the sills and a cane sofa" width="900" height="634" loading="lazy" />

Inside, plenty of these bays still have their original leaded-light glazing, which is one more reason to keep water away from the frames. The hood itself is pitched shallower than the main roof, often below what clay plain tiles really want. It was usually laid with no underlay at all, and the joint where it meets the brickwork under the upstairs windows was finished with a sand and cement fillet rather than lead. Ninety years on, the fillet has cracked away from the wall and the tile nails have rusted, so tiles start slipping one at a time.

The flat-topped porch is the other regular. Many 1930s houses had an open recessed porch that a later owner enclosed or extended, with a flat roof of felt on chipboard. Chipboard swells and falls apart once it gets wet. ## Why bay and porch roofs leak: the four usual faults

Almost every bay or porch leak we trace in Croydon comes back to one of four things, and they often turn up in pairs.

<img src="/images/blog/canted-bay-window-living-room-inside-view-croydon.webp" alt="A bright living room with a three-sided bay window and a radiator under the centre window" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

### Split or stolen lead

Lead on a bay top expands in sun and shrinks at night. If it was laid in one piece that's too big, or nailed down too tightly, it cracks along the fixings or at a fold. Lead on a ground-floor bay is also within reach from the front garden wall, which makes it a target for thieves.

### The abutment joint

Where the bay or porch roof meets the house wall, rain runs down the brickwork and has to be turned out over the roof. A lead flashing tucked into a mortar joint does that. A cement fillet does it for a while, then shrinks away from the wall and lets water run straight behind it.

### Rotten timber

Once the deck under a small roof has been wet for long enough, it loses its strength. Tiles won't hold their nails, lead dips and holds water, and on a two-storey bay the rot can spread into the head timber that carries the wall above.

### Failed felt

Felted porch and bay tops last roughly fifteen to twenty years. After that the surface crazes, the edges lift where they turn down into the gutter, and the joint against the wall opens. Patched more than twice? It's probably close to the end.

## Repair or rebuild: where the line usually falls

Most bay and porch roofs can be repaired, provided the timber underneath is sound. We work to one rule: renew the covering as often as you like, but get the rotten structure out first.

<img src="/images/blog/rotten-timber-board-rusted-nail-bay-roof-deck-croydon.webp" alt="Close-up of a split, decayed timber board with a rusted nail standing out of it" width="900" height="567" loading="lazy" />

A repair makes sense when the leak is in one place: a split in the lead, a few slipped tiles, a flashing that has worked loose. Lifting the covering on a bay top and finding a dry, firm deck means a new sheet of code 5 lead, or fresh tiles and underlay, will last decades. For the abutment, replacing a cement fillet with proper lead soakers and a cover flashing is nearly always worth doing while the roof is open.

Rebuilding becomes the better spend when the deck is soft, the joists have rotted at their ends, or the brickwork above the bay has started to crack or drop. At that point, re-covering a rotten bay is a waste of the lead. The job turns into carpentry: propping the wall above, cutting out the decayed head timber, putting in new joists and a new deck, then the covering. It's slower and dearer. It's also the only version that lasts. With a felted porch, rebuilding is often the cheaper answer anyway, because a modern single-ply or GRP covering on a new deck costs little more than a third coat of felt.

## Croydon Council, conservation areas and porch rules

Repairing a bay or porch roof with the same material doesn't need planning permission. Changing its shape, its covering or adding a new porch might, and Croydon has more places where that matters than people expect.

<img src="/images/blog/period-terrace-front-doors-fanlights-railings-london.webp" alt="Two black panelled front doors with arched fanlights, stone steps and iron railings on a period London terrace" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

The council lists 21 conservation areas, and several are made up of exactly the kind of houses that have period bays: South Norwood, Harold Road and Church Road in Upper Norwood, and Chatsworth Road in Addiscombe among them. You can check yours on <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning-policy/conservation-and-heritage/conservation-areas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Croydon Council's conservation areas page</a>. Inside one, the council expects front elevations to keep their character, so swapping a lead bay top for felt, or slate for concrete tiles, is the sort of change that draws attention. In the Chatsworth Road conservation area in Addiscombe, an Article 4 direction removes permitted development rights for roof alterations, so ask before changing anything there.

New porches have their own rule. Under the national <a href="https://www.planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/porches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Planning Portal guidance on porches</a>, a porch on a house doesn't need permission if its ground floor area stays within three square metres, no part goes above three metres, and it sits at least two metres back from a boundary facing a road. That allowance doesn't apply to flats, so a converted house in Thornton Heath or South Norwood with a shared front door is in a different position from the semi next door. If the porch is being rebuilt rather than repaired, it is worth checking before work starts.

## Access and 2026 prices for bay and porch roofs in Croydon

On a small roof, access can cost as much as the roofing. A ground-floor bay or a porch can usually be done from ladders or a small tower. A two-storey bay top needs a tower or a short scaffold, and in a terraced street where the bay sits a metre or two from the pavement, that scaffold often stands on the public footway and needs a council licence.
