The cheapest roofline quote you'll get in Croydon is nearly always a capping quote. Someone offers to cover your old timber fascias with white plastic, clip new soffit boards underneath, hang new gutters and be gone by teatime. On some houses that's a perfectly good job. On others it buries rot that will cost you twice as much in five years. Fascias, soffits and bargeboards in Croydon come in three broad generations: painted timber barges on Victorian and Edwardian gables, long hipped eaves on the 1930s semis, and thin flat edges on the estates of the 1960s and 70s. Each fails in its own way, and that decides whether repair, full replacement or capping is the right answer. There's also law involved. Birds nesting at the eaves are protected while the nest is in use, bat roosts are protected outright, and plenty of houses from the 1960s and 70s have soffit boards that should be tested for asbestos before anyone touches them. Here's how to work out which situation you're in, and roughly what each route costs in 2026.

## Fascias, soffits and bargeboards in Croydon: what each board is for

Three boards, three jobs. The fascia runs along the bottom edge of the roof, fixed to the ends of the rafters, and carries the gutter. The soffit closes the gap underneath, between the back of the fascia and the wall. The bargeboard is the sloping board that runs up the edge of a gable and covers the ends of the roof timbers where the tiles stop.

<img src="/images/roof-repairs-croydon-site-dormer-window-on-tiled-roof-croydon-w800.webp" alt="Small timber dormer with a painted fascia board and a gutter along its front edge, set into a steep tiled roof" width="800" height="532" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

Not every house has all three. Plenty of Victorian terraces in Thornton Heath and Selhurst have little or no soffit, with the gutter carried on brackets or on a projecting course of brick. A hipped 1930s semi has fascia and soffit right the way round and no bargeboard, unless it has a front gable. A gabled villa may have a bargeboard bigger and more decorative than anything else on the front of the house.

What they share is that they're the most exposed timber on the building. The fascia sits behind a gutter that overflows every time it blocks. The bargeboard takes driven rain on its face and wind on its edge. When something goes wrong at the roof edge, it's usually one of these boards that goes first. Our [fascia and soffit repair service in Croydon](/fascias-and-soffits) covers all three, and the first visit is mostly about working out which one is actually failing and why.

## How the roofline fails on Croydon's three main kinds of house

The age of the house tells you a lot about what you'll find.

<img src="/images/blog/rendered-gable-chimney-gutter-eaves-croydon-roofline.webp" alt="Black and white view of a rendered gable end with a brick chimney, a tiled roof and a gutter running along the eaves" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

### Victorian and Edwardian gables: South Norwood and Addiscombe

The older streets around South Norwood and Addiscombe have a lot of gabled fronts, and many still carry their original bargeboards. Some are plain planks. Others are cut into scallops, trefoils or pierced patterns. The red brick Arts and Crafts villas with front gables on Havelock Road and Elgin Road sit inside the East India Estate conservation area, where keeping that detail matters more than usual. These boards are often thin softwood, and the decoration gives water more places to sit. They rot at the bottom ends first, where the board meets the eaves and rainwater runs down the face and collects.

The answer here is rarely plastic. A joiner can copy a decorative bargeboard from a template taken off the old one, and a sound board with a rotten foot can often be spliced and saved.

### 1930s hipped semis: Shirley, Purley and the southern suburbs

The interwar semis that fill Shirley, Purley, Selsdon and Coulsdon have long, deep eaves that run right round a hipped roof, with a smaller run over the bay. That's a lot of timber per house, and most of it went up before the Second World War. Rot here almost always starts behind a gutter that has been leaking at a joint or overflowing at a corner for years. The corners of the hip and the little roof over the bay are where to look first.

### 1960s and 70s estates: New Addington, Forestdale and the Wates houses

Postwar building brought thinner, flatter roof edges. New Addington's council houses have shallow concrete tile roofs with plain fascias. Forestdale was largely built by Wates in the 1970s, and the same firm put up flat-roofed houses elsewhere in the borough, at Turnpike Lane in the late 1960s and around Tipton Drive and Hunters Way in the early 1970s. On houses like those, a deep timber fascia runs round the edge of a flat roof. On a flat roof the fascia and the waterproofing are one detail, so a split at the drip edge lets water straight into the board. These are also the houses where the soffit board itself may not be timber at all, which comes up again below.

## Rot or just old paint? Check before anyone quotes

Peeling paint on a fascia looks alarming from the pavement and often means very little. Timber can lose its paint and still be perfectly sound underneath, and a rub down and two coats will see it through another five years or more.

<img src="/images/blog/weathered-painted-timber-boards-peeling-paint-croydon-fascia.webp" alt="Weathered timber boards with pale paint flaking off to show grey bare wood underneath" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

The test is simple, and it's worth watching anyone who quotes you do it. Push a screwdriver or bradawl into the bottom edge of the board and into the ends, where the timber meets the next length or turns a corner. Sound wood resists. Rotten wood lets the tip sink in with hardly any effort, and it often comes out with damp brown fibres on it. Pay most attention to the area behind gutter joints, the corners of a hip, and the bottom few inches of each bargeboard.

Other signs point the same way. Gutter brackets that have pulled forward, or a gutter that has dropped out of line, usually mean the screws have nothing left to grip. Green or black streaks running down the face of the board show water getting behind the gutter. A soffit board that has sagged or come away from the wall often has a wet fascia above it.

If the rot is limited to a short length, cutting it out and scarf-jointing in new timber is a proper repair and much cheaper than replacing the whole run. If the screwdriver goes in every metre or so, the board has reached the end of its life.

## Capping, replacing or repairing fascias and soffits in Croydon

Capping means fixing thin uPVC cover boards over the existing timber fascia, and usually new uPVC soffit board underneath, without taking anything off. Full replacement means taking the old boards off and fixing new ones, timber or uPVC, straight onto the rafter ends.

<img src="/images/blog/rotted-ends-timber-boards-capping-fascia-croydon.webp" alt="Close view of upright timber boards whose ends have rotted away into soft, crumbling fibres" width="800" height="550" loading="lazy" />

Capping is cheaper and quicker. From the street it looks just as tidy. It's a reasonable choice in one situation: when the timber underneath is sound and dry along the whole run, and you'd rather never paint it again. The cover board is cosmetic. It carries no load, so the gutter brackets still go through it into the original wood.

That's also where it goes wrong. Cap over timber that is already damp and the plastic keeps the rain off the face but traps the moisture already inside. Rot keeps going, out of sight, until the gutter brackets pull out of soft wood. By then you're paying to take off the capping, the rotten board and often the rafter ends behind it, which costs more than replacing the board in the first place would have. If you're offered capping, ask to see the bradawl test along the full length before you agree, and ask what happens if they find soft timber once they start. We'd sooner talk someone out of a capping job than fix plastic over wood that's already going.

Full replacement in uPVC suits most 1930s semis and postwar houses. Nothing period is lost and the boards need no decorating. On Victorian and Edwardian gables, and anywhere in a conservation area, painted timber is often the better answer and sometimes the only acceptable one. ## Vented soffits and the loft above them

A roof needs air moving through it. On most pitched roofs that air gets in at the eaves, through the soffit or over the fascia, and leaves near the ridge. It carries away the warm, damp air that rises out of the house before it can condense on the cold underside of the roof.

<img src="/images/blog/condensation-droplets-cold-surface-croydon-loft-soffit-vents.webp" alt="Dense droplets of condensation covering a cold surface" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Roofline work is where that airflow most often gets blocked. A solid uPVC soffit fixed over the old timber one, with no vents, closes the gap. Loft insulation pushed right into the eaves does the same from the inside. The result shows up a winter or two later as drips on the felt, black mould on the rafters and damp patches at the edges of the loft. We've gone through the symptoms and the fixes in our article on [loft condensation in Croydon roofs](/blog/loft-condensation-roof-ventilation-croydon).

So whatever you choose for the fascias and soffits, the quote should say how the eaves will be ventilated. That might be vented soffit boards, a continuous strip vent, or an over-fascia vent that sits behind the gutter, plus eaves trays or spacers in the loft that hold the insulation back from the airway. If the quote says nothing about ventilation, ask.

## Birds, bats and asbestos: when roofline work has to wait

Some roofline jobs in Croydon stall before a single board comes off, and the reason has nothing to do with the timber.

<img src="/images/blog/house-martin-chicks-mud-nest-eaves-croydon.webp" alt="Two house martin chicks looking out of a mud nest built under the eaves of a building" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

### Nesting birds under the eaves

House martins build mud nests under the eaves, and swifts and house sparrows use gaps behind fascias and soffits. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally take, damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. In practice, if birds are nesting on a stretch of roofline between spring and late summer, work on that stretch waits until the young have flown. Booking roofline work in autumn or winter avoids it.

### Bats behind the boards

Pipistrelles and other bats squeeze into tiny gaps behind fascias, bargeboards and soffits, especially on older houses near parks and woods. All bat species and their roosts are protected, and the government's guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/bats-protection-surveys-and-licences" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">bat protection, surveys and licences</a> lists roof repairs among the work that can affect them. Damaging a roost can mean an unlimited fine or up to six months in prison. If there are droppings stuck to the wall below the eaves, or bats are seen leaving at dusk, the job stops until a bat ecologist has been out.

### Asbestos soffit boards

Houses built or refurbished from the 1950s into the 1980s, a description that fits plenty of homes in New Addington and Forestdale, sometimes have flat grey-white soffit boards that aren't timber or plastic. HSE's <a href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/asbestos/diagrams.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">diagrams of where asbestos is found in buildings</a> list asbestos insulating board and asbestos cement soffits and fascias among the materials to look out for in homes built before 2000. Get a sample tested before anyone cuts, drills or prises the boards off. Asbestos cement is usually non-licensed work, but it still has to come down whole and bagged, never snapped or cut. Asbestos insulating board is softer and gives off fibres far more easily, and removing it is usually a job for a licensed contractor.

## Access, Croydon Council and 2026 prices

Access often costs more than the boards. A ladder is fine for a look, but not for a day spent cutting and fixing at the eaves. A mobile tower suits a single elevation of a two-storey house, a bay roof or a short run, and can be put up and taken down in a few hours. A full roofline on a hipped semi, or anything over a conservatory, a steep drive or a front garden too small for a tower, usually needs scaffold. If that scaffold stands on the pavement, Croydon Council needs to license it, which adds time as well as cost.

<img src="/images/blog/scaffold-boards-ladder-access-croydon-roofline-work.webp" alt="Steel scaffold with boarded lifts and a ladder set up against the wall of a building" width="900" height="429" loading="lazy" />

Permission is rarely an issue. Fascia and soffit work counts as maintenance on most houses in the borough. The exception is the Chatsworth Road conservation area in Addiscombe, where the <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning-policy/article-4-directions/chatsworth-road-conservation-area" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Chatsworth Road Article 4 direction</a> removes the right to make any alteration to the roof of a house and the right to paint the outside of a building. Swapping timber for uPVC or changing the colour there needs a word with the council first. Croydon's other 20 conservation areas have no Article 4 on roofs, but changing material on a street-facing gable is still worth checking.
