When rain gets into a Croydon house through the roof, it rarely comes through the middle of a slope. It comes in where the roof stops: against a chimney, along a party wall, down the line where two slopes meet, or at the wall above a bay window. Those joints are sealed with lead flashing, lead-lined valleys or, on cheaper repairs, a strip of sand and cement, and they fail long before the tiles or slates do. This guide shows how to work out which junction is leaking from the stain inside, what a proper repair involves, and what lead flashing and valley repairs cost in Croydon in 2026. A few numbers worth knowing first: Code 4 lead, the standard for most flashings, is 1.80mm thick and weighs about 20kg per square metre, and a single piece should not run much more than a metre and a half before it is lapped onto the next.

## Reading the stain: which junction is leaking on a Croydon house

Before anyone goes up a ladder, the ceiling and walls tell you a lot. Lead and valley leaks put water in predictable places, and on Croydon's Victorian and Edwardian housing those places are much the same from one street to the next.

<img src="/images/roof-repairs-croydon-site-tall-red-chimney-pots-stone-roof-croydon-w800.webp" alt="Brick chimney stack rising from a slate roof with lead-lined valleys where the lower roof slopes meet, the junctions that leak most on Croydon houses" width="800" height="522" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

A damp patch on the chimney breast in the top front bedroom, often worse after south-westerly rain, points to the flashings around the stack. A wet line along the top of the wall you share with next door, on a terrace in Selhurst or South Norwood, points to the abutment where your roof meets the party wall. A brown stain in the corner of a back bedroom, where the rear wing joins the main house, is nearly always the junction over the back addition. And water appearing at the head of a bay window, sometimes running down inside the frame, is the small lead roof over the bay or the flashing where it meets the front wall.

None of this is proof. Condensation, a cracked pot or a blocked gutter can leave similar marks. But if the stain hugs a wall and only shows up in wind-driven rain, flashing is the first suspect. ## Chimney stacks and party walls: two abutments every terrace has

An abutment is anywhere a roof slope runs into a wall that rises above it. Every chimney stack has four sides of abutment, and on a terrace where the party walls are carried up through the roof as a low brick parapet, each house has a long one on either side as well.

<img src="/images/blog/chimney-stacks-party-wall-parapets-terrace-rooftops-croydon.webp" alt="Terraced rooftops with brick party wall parapets and chimney stacks rising through the slopes, the abutments where flashing leaks start on Croydon terraces" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Done properly, the side of a stack or parapet has a soaker under each tile or slate, turned up the wall, with a stepped cover flashing over the top that is wedged and pointed into the brick joints. The front of the stack gets an apron dressed down over the tiles. The back, where the slope runs straight into the brickwork, needs a small back gutter to carry water round the sides. That back gutter is where we find the most trouble on Croydon stacks, because it holds leaves, it sits in the shade, and on a lot of houses it was never there in the first place.

Usually the fault is small and boring. The mortar holding the flashing into the joint dries out and the lead lifts a few millimetres. The wedges work loose. Someone has repointed the stack and smeared fresh mortar over the lead instead of raking it out and re-tucking it. We cover the stack itself, whether to repoint, rebuild or take it down, in [our chimney stacks guide](/blog/chimney-stacks-croydon-repoint-rebuild-cap-remove). This section is only about the lead that seals it to the roof.

## Back-addition valleys: two slopes, one channel

Valleys collect more water than any other part of a roof, because two slopes drain into one channel. On Croydon terraces you get them in two places: where the main roof meets the roof of the back addition, and between two neighbouring back additions that slope towards each other.

<img src="/images/blog/slate-roof-dormer-valleys-lead-croydon.webp" alt="Grey slate roof with several dormers, each one creating a pair of valleys where the slopes meet" width="900" height="506" loading="lazy" />

A lead valley is laid on boards between the rafters, with the tiles or slates cut and lapped over it on each side. The lead itself often outlasts the timber underneath. Water that has crept past a split, or condensation forming under the lead in winter, softens the boards until the lead sags, and once it sags it holds water instead of shedding it. Leaves from the gardens behind Thornton Heath and Addiscombe make that worse every autumn. The shared gutter between two back additions brings the extra problem of who pays, which [our back addition roof guide](/blog/outrigger-back-addition-roofs-croydon-victorian-terraces) covers alongside the rest of that rear slope.

## The lead over a bay window

Bay windows are the junction people forget. The Edwardian semis and terraces in Norbury, Thornton Heath and around Upper Norwood have a lot of two storey bays, some with a small lead flat on top and some with a little tiled hipped roof. Either way there is a flashing where the bay meets the front wall, and because the bay sticks out into the weather, that flashing takes rain from three directions.

<img src="/images/blog/edwardian-red-brick-bay-windows-lead-bay-roofs-croydon.webp" alt="Red brick Edwardian building with projecting bay windows and a gabled roof, the kind of bay roof junction that leaks on older Croydon houses" width="900" height="675" loading="lazy" />

A leaking bay roof often gets misdiagnosed as a window problem. If the water appears at the top of the frame after heavy rain and the window has been resealed once already, have someone look at the lead above it.

## Mortar fillets: why the cheap fix keeps coming back

A lot of junctions in Croydon were never leaded at all, or were leaded and later "repaired" with a wedge of sand and cement trowelled into the angle between roof and wall. That wedge is a fillet. Builders used them on cheaper Victorian work and they turn up constantly on back additions, porch roofs and the sides of party wall parapets.

<img src="/images/blog/eroded-mortar-joints-brickwork-cement-fillet-croydon.webp" alt="Close view of weathered brickwork with eroded and cracked mortar joints, the same kind of failure that opens up cement fillets at roof abutments in Croydon" width="800" height="600" loading="lazy" />

The trouble is movement. The wall, the roof timbers and the tiles all expand, shrink and settle at different rates, and a rigid strip of mortar spanning between them has nowhere to give. It cracks along its length, then shrinks back from the brick, and water runs into the gap and straight down the inside face of the wall. Patching it with more mortar, or with a tube of sealant, buys a season or two.

Lead doesn't have that problem, because it bends. Soakers under each course move with the tiles, and the cover flashing above moves with the wall, so the two can shift past each other without opening a gap. If your quote says "rake out and renew fillet", ask what it would cost to replace it with lead instead. On most abutments the difference is a few hundred pounds, and the lead version should still be there in thirty years.

## Lead flashing codes and piece lengths: what should be on the quote

Lead sheet is sold by code, which is a rough guide to its weight. Code 3 is 1.32mm thick, Code 4 is 1.80mm, Code 5 is 2.24mm and Code 6 is 2.65mm. Thicker lead is stiffer, lasts longer and copes better with foot traffic and standing water, but it costs more and is harder to dress into tight shapes.

<img src="/images/blog/caliper-measuring-sheet-metal-thickness-lead-code-croydon.webp" alt="Digital caliper measuring the thickness of a sheet of metal, the measurement that separates one code of lead from the next" width="900" height="601" loading="lazy" />

As a rule of thumb, soakers are Code 3 or Code 4, stepped and cover flashings are Code 4, and valleys and chimney back gutters are Code 4 or Code 5, with Code 5 the better choice where a lot of water collects. If a roofer quotes for a valley in Code 3, that is a corner being cut.

Length matters as much as thickness. Lead expands and contracts noticeably as it heats and cools, and a long piece fixed at both ends tears itself apart along the fixings. Each piece should be short, lapped over the next by at least 100mm, and fixed only at the top so the rest can move. After a hot summer like the ones south London has had recently, the splits we find are nearly always in pieces that were too long or nailed too often. The <a href="https://leadsheet.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Lead Sheet Training Academy</a> trains roofers to these standards, and it is reasonable to ask whoever is quoting where they learned their leadwork.

## Lead theft in Croydon and the alternatives to lead

Lead is valuable enough to steal, and low back-addition roofs and bay roofs reached from a garden wall or a flat-roofed extension are easy targets. Thieves usually take the long, easy pieces: valley linings, aprons, and the lead from bay tops. You tend to find out at the next downpour.

<img src="/images/blog/scrap-metal-yard-grab-lead-theft-croydon.webp" alt="A mechanical grab lifting scrap metal at a recycling yard, where stolen roof lead usually ends up" width="600" height="400" loading="lazy" />

The law has made it harder to sell. Under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/10/contents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Scrap Metal Dealers Act 2013</a>, scrap dealers must be licensed by the council and cannot pay cash. It still happens, though, and it goes up when scrap prices do.

If your lead has gone, or you would rather not put back something worth stealing, there are alternatives. GRP (glass fibre) valleys come pre-formed and are common on concrete-tiled 1930s roofs. Flexible lead substitutes made from polymer or aluminium-backed materials can be dressed like lead and have no scrap value. They are lighter and cheaper to fit, but they do not last as long as properly laid lead, and in one of Croydon's conservation areas a visible change from lead on a front elevation may not be welcome. On a back addition nobody can see from the road, a substitute is a sensible choice. On the front of a period house, lead is still usually the right answer, with the pieces marked with a forensic marking product and a note to your insurer.

### What pushes the price up

Scaffold on a two storey elevation in Croydon costs £500 to £1,200, and it needs a licence from the council if it stands on the pavement. Back-addition and bay work can often be done from a tower or a ladder, which saves a lot. Rotten boards under a valley, a stack that needs repointing before it can be reflashed, and a neighbour's half of a shared gutter all turn up once the old lead comes off. A good quote names the lead code, says whether the boards are included, and lists access as its own line.
