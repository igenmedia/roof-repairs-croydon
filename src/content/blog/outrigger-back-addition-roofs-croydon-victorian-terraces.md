Stand in any back garden in Thornton Heath, South Norwood, Broad Green, Addiscombe or Waddon and look up at the house. You will see two roofs, not one. The main roof sits high and steep over the front rooms. Then, poking out towards you, there is a narrower two-storey wing with its own lower roof - the outrigger, or back addition, which the Victorian builder put the kitchen and the scullery under, with the bathroom or box room above. Croydon has a little over 150,000 households and a huge share of the older ones are terraces of exactly this type, built between about 1880 and 1914. When we get a call about a leak on one of those houses, the back addition is the culprit more often than the main roof and the chimney put together. Its slope is shallower, its junctions are more awkward, and in many cases it is the only roof on the house that nobody has ever re-covered. The rest of this explains why, and what each fix costs.

## What the back addition is, and why Croydon has so many

The outrigger was a plan-form solution to a narrow plot. A terrace frontage of fifteen or sixteen feet does not leave room for a kitchen alongside two reception rooms, so the builder pushed the service rooms out the back in a wing that took up roughly half the width of the house, leaving a strip of yard beside it for light and a side door. Upstairs the wing carried a third bedroom or, after the 1890s, a proper bathroom. Pairs of houses were usually built as mirror images, so two outriggers sit side by side along the boundary with the yards on the outside - which is the detail that creates the shared gutter we will come to.

Croydon's railway suburbs were built almost entirely to this pattern. The streets off London Road through Broad Green, the grid between Thornton Heath station and Grange Park, the long Victorian runs around Portland Road in South Norwood, the older half of Addiscombe and the terraces off Purley Way in Waddon - all of them are two-roof houses. Our [roof leak repair](/roof-leak-repairs) work in the borough is, in large part, back addition work, and it is worth understanding why the little roof fails more than the big one before you pay anyone to go up there.

## Why the rear slope gives up before the main roof does

The main roof on a Croydon terrace was built at somewhere between 35 and 45 degrees, which suits slate and suits clay plain tile. The outrigger roof had to stay below the first-floor windows of the main house, so the builder flattened it to whatever pitch fitted - commonly 25 to 30 degrees, sometimes less on the final lean-to over the scullery. He then covered it in the same slate as the front, laid at the same lap, and that is the original sin. Small Welsh slates at a shallow pitch with a short head lap let wind-driven rain creep up between the courses, and a south-westerly storm hitting the back of a Thornton Heath terrace is exactly that kind of rain.

<img src="/images/blog/terraced-houses-rear-additions-aerial-croydon.webp" alt="Top-down view of a row of terraced houses showing the lower rear addition roofs projecting into the back gardens" width="900" height="506" loading="lazy" />

Age compounds it. When the main roof was re-covered in the 1960s or 1980s, which most of them were, the outrigger was frequently left alone because it was cheaper, out of sight and "still fine". So the back addition is now carrying 110 to 140 year old slate on original battens with no underlay at all, nailed with iron nails that have rusted to nothing. Nail sickness, where slates slide out because the fixings have gone rather than because the slate has failed, is the single most common thing we find on these roofs. If the covering is clay rather than slate, the pitch problem is sharper still, because a plain tile really wants 35 degrees and the back addition rarely gives it that - we went into the tile side of this in our piece on [clay plain tiles on Croydon's older roofs](/blog/clay-plain-tiles-croydon-victorian-roofs).

### Three junctions the main roof never has to deal with

A main terrace roof has a ridge, two slopes, a verge or two and a chimney. The outrigger roof has all of that plus an abutment where it meets the rear wall of the main house, a gutter where it meets next door's outrigger, and a step down to the scullery lean-to. Every one of those is a change of plane, every change of plane relies on lead and timber rather than slate, and lead and timber are what fail.

## The gutter between two outriggers, and who pays for it

Where two back additions were built tight against each other, their roofs slope towards each other and meet in a gutter that runs from the rear wall of the houses out to the garden end. Sometimes it is a proper lead-lined box gutter on a timber sole board. Sometimes it is a lead valley laid on boards. Either way it is low, flat, hidden from both gardens and the first place every leaf from every garden tree ends up. One blocked outlet in November and the water backs up over the lead and into the ceiling of whichever bathroom is lower.

<img src="/images/blog/shared-gutter-back-additions-terrace-rooftops-croydon.webp" alt="Rooftops of terraced houses seen from above with paired rear additions meeting along the boundary" width="640" height="480" loading="lazy" />

That gutter sits on, or straddles, the boundary line, which makes it a shared structure in all but the most unusual freehold arrangements. In practice the cost of relining it is split, most commonly half and half, and the sensible thing is to agree that before anyone goes up. The <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/preventing-and-resolving-disputes-in-relation-to-party-walls" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">government's explanatory booklet on the Party Wall etc. Act 1996</a> covers work to shared structures and the notice you owe your neighbour before cutting into one. For a straightforward reline you will rarely need a surveyor, but you will need a conversation, because the roofer has to stand on both roofs and the scaffold will probably be in both yards.

We see a particular pattern on converted houses here. Where one of the pair has been split into flats and the other is still a single home, the freeholder of the flats and the owner-occupier next door frequently have different ideas about urgency. The leak, meanwhile, is dripping into whichever property is lower.

## The faults we find most often on a Croydon back addition

Walk a dozen of these roofs and the same list comes up every time. Slipped and missing slates on the low slope, nearly always from nail sickness rather than broken slate. Rotten valley boards and gutter sole boards, because the lead over them failed years ago and the timber has been wet ever since. A blocked or undersized central gutter outlet, often a single two-inch hole that was never enough. Failed lead soakers and a cracked cement fillet at the abutment to the rear wall of the main house, so rain running down the brickwork gets behind the top course of slates. And underneath it all, no underlay, so every one of those faults goes straight through to the lath and plaster ceiling.

<img src="/images/blog/slate-roof-abutment-dormer-brick-wall-croydon.webp" alt="Slate roof slope meeting a taller brick wall at an abutment, the junction that fails on back addition roofs" width="900" height="601" loading="lazy" />

The scullery lean-to at the far end deserves a paragraph of its own. It is the lowest roof on the house, often under 20 degrees, usually covered in whatever the builder had left, and it has been patched by every owner since with a bucket of bitumen. By the time we see it, there can be three generations of flashband over a lean-to that needs a new deck. Where the original covering has been swapped for felt or a sheet product, it is really a flat roof and should be treated as one - our page on [flat roof repairs](/flat-roof-repairs) covers what that involves.

### How to tell which roof is leaking

Stand in the room with the stain and work out which roof is above it. A damp patch in the bathroom or back bedroom ceiling, or running down the inside of the wall between the back bedroom and the main house, is the outrigger or its abutment. A patch in the front or middle bedroom is the main roof. Water appearing at the top of the kitchen wall on the boundary side, with a matching patch next door, is the shared gutter. Obvious, you would think, but we are regularly asked to quote for a main roof when the problem is the little one behind it.

## Repair the fault or re-cover the whole wing: what it costs

Because the outrigger roof is small, a full re-cover of it is not the enormous job a main roof is, and that changes the arithmetic. A back addition on a typical Croydon terrace has two slopes totalling somewhere between 15 and 25 square metres, plus the gutter and the lean-to.

<img src="/images/blog/roofer-hammer-scaffold-outrigger-repair-croydon.webp" alt="Roofer working with a hammer on scaffolding during a roof repair" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Single-fault repairs are cheap on their own. Refixing a handful of slipped slates with copper tingles runs from about £180 to £400. Re-dressing or replacing lead soakers and the cement fillet at the abutment, a few metres of it, is usually £350 to £900. Clearing and relining the shared gutter in new lead, with a new sole board where the old one has rotted, falls between £900 and £2,200 depending on length and how much timber has gone, split with next door. A new deck and covering on the scullery lean-to is £800 to £1,800.

The catch is that these faults seldom come alone. A nail-sick slope with a rotten valley board and a cracked abutment fillet is three separate repairs with scaffold or a tower for each visit, and the slope will keep shedding slates between visits. Re-covering the outrigger as one job - stripping the slates, replacing battens, laying a breathable underlay, new lead to the abutment and the gutter, then relaying with the sound original slates made up with matching reclaimed ones - typically comes out between £4,500 and £8,500 for a Croydon terrace, with the scaffold inside that figure. It fixes every item on the list at once and leaves the one roof on the house that was never done in better shape than the one that was. If you are weighing those two paths, the decision is a smaller version of the one in our [repair or replace](/blog/repair-or-replace-croydon-roof) article, with the difference that the small roof tips towards replacement sooner.

## Loft conversions, L-shaped dormers and the planning picture

A good share of Croydon's back additions have already disappeared under a loft conversion. The L-shaped dormer, the borough's most common loft type, builds a flat-roofed box across the main roof and continues it out over the outrigger, which takes the old shallow slope and its three junctions out of the equation completely. If you are planning one, have the outrigger looked at first anyway: the dormer still lands on the rear wall and still drains towards the same shared gutter, and a rotten wall plate found after the slates are off is an expensive surprise.

<img src="/images/blog/terraced-streets-loft-dormers-aerial-croydon.webp" alt="Aerial view of long rows of terraced streets of the kind where rear dormer loft conversions are common" width="800" height="450" loading="lazy" />

On the planning side, Croydon Council is the authority for all of the areas named in this article, and whether you need permission for either a re-cover or a loft depends mostly on where the house sits. Croydon's <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning-policy/conservation-and-heritage/conservation-areas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">conservation areas list</a> is the first check, because several of them carry Article 4 directions that pull roof alterations out of permitted development. The <a href="https://www.planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/loft-conversion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Planning Portal's loft conversion guidance</a> sets out the volume and materials limits that apply everywhere else. We have written up the Croydon-specific detail, including the Article 4 position, in our guide to [planning permission for roof work in Croydon](/blog/planning-permission-roof-work-croydon), so we will not repeat it here. The short version for a like-for-like re-cover of an outrigger in matching slate: almost never an application. For a change of material on a conservation area house: ask first.

## When it goes wrong: heat in July, storms in January

Outrigger roofs fail on a schedule, and it is worth knowing it. The summer does the damage quietly. Weeks of sun on a shallow, exposed rear slope cook the lead at the abutment and the gutter, open the laps on any felt lean-to and crack the cement fillet, which we covered in more depth in [what a London summer does to a Croydon roof](/blog/london-summer-heat-croydon-roofs). Then autumn drops every leaf from the garden trees into the shared gutter, and the first proper south-westerly of the winter drives rain up the low slope and over the backed-up lead at the same time. The call usually comes in the week after the storm, from the bathroom ceiling.

<img src="/images/blog/storm-rain-dripping-roof-edge-croydon.webp" alt="Heavy rain running off the edge of a roof during a storm" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Which gives you the calendar. Have the shared gutter cleared and the outrigger looked over in September or October, after the heat and before the leaves. It is a short visit on a short roof, and it is the difference between a cleared outlet and a new bathroom ceiling. If the slope is nail-sick, the survey will tell you so, and you can plan the re-cover for the spring rather than meet it as an emergency in January.
