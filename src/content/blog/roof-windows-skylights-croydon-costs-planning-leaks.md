A hole in the roof with glass in it is the easiest way to get daylight into a loft, a landing or a dark back bedroom. It is also one of the places a Croydon roof is most likely to let water in. Most roof windows here are fitted without a planning application, provided the glass sits within 150mm of the roof slope, but Croydon Council lists 21 conservation areas, and one of them in Addiscombe takes away that right altogether. The cost runs from a few hundred pounds for a sun tunnel to well over £3,000 for a conservation rooflight in a new opening. And whether the window stays dry for thirty years depends less on the brand than on how the tiles around it were cut, lapped and dressed. Here is how the three types differ, where each kind of Croydon roof makes the job harder, and what people are paying in 2026.

## Roof window, fixed rooflight or sun tunnel?

Three products get called "skylights", and they do different jobs. A roof window is the one most people picture: a timber or white-coated frame set into the slope with a sash that pivots so you can open it, clean the outside and get air through the room. It is the usual choice for a loft room, a converted attic bedroom or a bathroom under the eaves. A fixed rooflight doesn't open. That makes it simpler and slightly cheaper, and it suits stairwells and spaces you only want lit. The slim conservation rooflights, with a dark frame and a central glazing bar, are made to look like the old cast-iron ones on Victorian roofs.

<img src="/images/roof-repairs-croydon-site-slate-roof-rooflight-croydon-leak-repairs-w1200.webp" alt="Black and white photo of a dark tiled roof with one roof window set into the slope and a line of pigeons along the ridge" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

A sun tunnel is the odd one out. A small dome on the roof feeds a mirrored tube down through the loft to a round diffuser in a ceiling. You can't see out of it or open it, but it puts daylight into a room that has no roof slope above it. The classic case is the windowless landing in the middle of a 1930s semi. Common sizes are 250mm and 350mm across, and the larger one is worth having if the tube has to bend.

If you already have one of these and water is coming in around it, that is a repair, not a new installation. Our [roof leak repairs page](/roof-leak-repairs) covers how we trace that, and the rest of this post explains what usually turns out to be the cause.

## Leak or condensation? Reading the stain

Before anyone climbs a ladder, work out what kind of wet you have. In winter, a lot of "leaking Velux" worries turn out to be condensation, and the fix for that is nothing like the fix for a leak.

<img src="/images/blog/condensation-droplets-on-cold-glass-croydon.webp" alt="Condensation droplets running down the inside of cold window glass with warm light behind" width="900" height="580" loading="lazy" />

Condensation turns up on cold, still mornings, mostly in bathrooms and bedrooms where people breathe and shower all night with the window shut. The water forms on the inside of the glass and on the cold frame, runs down, and soaks the bottom edge of the plaster lining. Over a few winters it rots the bottom rail of an old pine frame. A real leak keeps time with the weather instead. It shows after wind-driven rain from the south-west, or after a summer cloudburst when the channel at the head of the window overflows. Usually the stain starts at a top corner or runs down one side of the reveal, well away from the bottom of the glass.

The third possibility is misting between the two panes. That means the sealed glazing unit has failed. It won't make a ceiling wet, but it won't clear either, and the only cure is a new glazing unit or a new sash.

Croydon's trees cause one problem of their own. Through Purley, Kenley, Sanderstead and Shirley, leaves and seed pods collect in the gap above a roof window every autumn. They dam the water so it backs up under the top of the flashing. A window that only leaks from October to December, and only on the slope under the oak, has probably told you its cause already.

## Croydon's three main roofs, and what each does to the job

The window is the same product whichever roof it goes in. The work around it is not.

<img src="/images/blog/aerial-suburban-semis-hipped-roofs-back-gardens-croydon.webp" alt="Black and white aerial view of suburban semi-detached houses with hipped tiled roofs and long back gardens" width="800" height="600" loading="lazy" />

### Victorian and Edwardian houses with clay plain tiles

Plenty of the older houses north of the town centre are slated, but the Edwardian streets and the Arts and Crafts houses further south are often covered in small clay plain tiles, laid double-lapped on a steep pitch. Each course is only a few inches deep, so a tidy job needs tile-and-a-half tiles at the sides of the window rather than slivers of cut tile, and a soaker slipped under every course. A good number went on with nothing underneath but the battens, since roofing felt only became usual after the war. Cutting a hole in one means adding a collar of underlay around the opening, fixed so that it drains onto the battens and doesn't sit in a pocket behind the window.

### 1930s semis in Shirley, Selsdon, Sanderstead and Coulsdon

The interwar semis usually have hipped roofs, so the rear slope narrows towards the top and the side slopes are short. A window has to sit low enough to clear the hip rafters, and anything wider than the gap between two rafters means cutting one and trimming either side. A lot of these houses were re-roofed in concrete tiles decades ago, so check what is actually on yours before ordering a flashing kit. Don't go by what the street had in 1934.

### Post-war houses with concrete interlocking tiles

On the estates of New Addington and Fieldway, and on plenty of re-roofed houses elsewhere, the covering is large interlocking concrete tiles. They are quick to cut around, but a tile cut down the side of a window loses its interlock. It needs a clip or a mechanical fixing, or the first big gale will lift it. The lead apron along the bottom of the flashing also has to be dressed down into every trough of the tile profile below it. An apron left sitting flat across the tops of the waves is a very common fault on these roofs.

## The flashing kit decides whether it leaks

The glass and the frame almost never let water in. The flashing is the set of metal pieces that sits between the frame and the tiles, and that is where the problems are. Manufacturers make different kits for different coverings. Velux, for example, sells a plain-tile kit (EDP) built around separate soakers, a standard kit for profiled tiles (EDW), and a recessed kit (EDJ) that sets the window lower into the roof. The wrong kit on the right roof is a slow leak waiting for the right storm.

<img src="/images/blog/roof-windows-flashing-dark-profiled-tiles-croydon.webp" alt="Two roof windows with grey metal flashing set into a roof of dark profiled tiles, with a chimney behind" width="900" height="681" loading="lazy" />

The usual faults:

- A profiled-tile kit fitted to a plain-tile roof, so the side pieces sit over the tiles instead of being interleaved with them.
- Tiles at the head of the window not cut back far enough, leaving no clear gutter for water to run along and off the sides.
- The underlay collar missing, so water that gets past the tiles in a storm runs straight onto the lining.
- No insulation collar around the frame, which gives you a cold strip inside the reveal and a line of condensation right where it marks the paint.
- A previous "repair" made by squeezing sealant into the joints. It holds for a season, then cracks and traps water where it does the most harm.

If the window itself is sound, re-flashing it is often the better buy: lift the tiles around it, fit the correct kit and relay them. If the frame is rotten or the glass has failed, though, it makes sense to replace the window and the flashing together while the tiles are already off.

## Replacing an old roof window in the same opening

Lofts converted in the 1980s and 1990s often still have their original pine roof windows, and many of those are near the end of their life. Signs to look for are a bottom rail gone black and soft, a sash that won't close tight, hinges that grind, or glass that has misted between the panes.

<img src="/images/blog/roof-window-lined-reveal-sky-croydon.webp" alt="Looking up through a roof window opening lined with pale timber boards to a blue sky with cloud" width="900" height="599" loading="lazy" />

Usually the hole can stay as it is. The big manufacturers still make windows to fit openings cut decades ago. The size code is printed on a small data plate on the frame, which you can see when the sash is open, and that is the first thing to photograph before you ask for a price. A like-for-like replacement keeps the same rafters and trimmers, so most of the job happens from inside. It goes in with a new flashing kit and insulation collar, and the lining gets made good afterwards.

Choose the frame finish for the room. Pine is fine in a bedroom or study. In a bathroom or kitchen, a white polyurethane-coated frame shrugs off the steam that rots timber. If the window is the escape route from a loft room, check before ordering that the replacement still opens wide enough. Some newer styles open differently from the one you have.

## Planning permission: permitted development and Croydon's exceptions

Most roof windows on houses don't need an application. The national permitted development rules allow them if three conditions are met. The glass and frame must project no more than 150mm beyond the plane of the roof slope. Nothing can sit higher than the highest part of the roof. And a window on a side-facing slope must be obscure glazed and fixed shut, unless the opening part is more than 1.7 metres above the floor of the room. The <a href="https://interactive.planningportal.co.uk/detached-house/outside/roof-window" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Planning Portal's interactive guide to roof windows</a> sets these out with diagrams.

<img src="/images/blog/victorian-villas-tree-lined-street-front-slopes-planning-croydon.webp" alt="Tree-lined London street of Victorian villas behind brick front walls and railings, with a magnolia in flower" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" />

People often assume a front slope is off limits. Under the national rules it isn't: a flush roof window facing the street is treated the same as one at the back. Whether it looks right on a terrace of matching Victorian roofs is a separate question, and on those streets a conservation-style rooflight is usually the better neighbour.

Croydon's exceptions are where it matters. The Chatsworth Road conservation area in Addiscombe has an Article 4 direction that removes permitted development for all alterations to a house's roof, so there even one rooflight on the back needs an application. Other directions in the borough cover different things, and <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning-policy/article-4-directions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Croydon Council's Article 4 directions page</a> links to each one. Flats and maisonettes have no permitted development rights for roof alterations at all, which catches out a lot of owners of converted Victorian houses in Thornton Heath, South Norwood and Selhurst. Listed buildings need listed building consent whatever the window. And if you are not sure, a Lawful Development Certificate gives you proof that the work didn't need permission. That is useful when you come to sell.

## Building regulations: who signs it off

Planning and building regulations are separate questions, and the second one applies more often. Cutting a new opening means cutting or trimming rafters. That is a structural alteration, and it normally needs building control involved, with an engineer's note on the trimming if the window is wide or the roof is already carrying a dormer.

<img src="/images/blog/attic-bedroom-two-roof-windows-building-regs-croydon.webp" alt="Loft bedroom with two roof windows in the sloping ceiling, a double bed and a glazed door to a balcony" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Replacing a whole roof window, frame and all, in an existing opening counts as a "controlled fitting". The new window has to meet the heat loss limits in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/conservation-of-fuel-and-power-approved-document-l" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Approved Document L of the building regulations</a>, which any current double-glazed roof window from a mainstream maker will. The installer either self-certifies through a competent person scheme such as FENSA or CERTASS, or you notify building control yourself. Swapping just a glazing unit or a sash is classed as a repair and sits outside both.

If a roof window serves a habitable loft room, Part B of the regulations may need it to work as an escape window. That means a clear opening of at least 0.33 square metres, at least 450mm high and 450mm wide, with the bottom of the opening no more than 1.1 metres above the floor. Our [post on loft conversion roofs in Croydon](/blog/loft-conversion-dormer-roofs-croydon) covers the dormer and structural side of a full conversion, which is a bigger job than adding a window.

A few things push the price up. A front slope over a busy road, or a three-storey Victorian house, may need a scaffold or a tower, which can add several hundred pounds. South London has no shortage of window fitters or roofers. Finding one who will do both properly is harder: someone who fits the frame and then relays the tiles around it the way the roof needs. Ask any installer which flashing kit they plan to use and how they will handle the tiles at the sides. A clear answer is a good sign.
