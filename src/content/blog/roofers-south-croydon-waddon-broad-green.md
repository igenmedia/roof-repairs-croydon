Give us your road name and we can usually tell you what's on your roof before we've seen it. In this corner of the borough that works better than almost anywhere else. Within about a mile and a half of East Croydon you can find slate villas from the 1850s, yellow-brick Gothic houses laid out between 1868 and 1890, terraces put up in 1902 on a sold-off private estate, and a council estate that Croydon started building in the 1920s. Each generation was roofed differently and each one fails in its own way. If you're looking for roofers in South Croydon, Waddon and Broad Green, the most useful thing you can know is roughly when your house was built. This guide goes through the area era by era. For each period it covers what the roofs are made of, what we tend to find wrong with them, which Croydon Council rules apply (there are two conservation areas here and one Article 4 direction that people misread) and what the work costs in 2026.

## Roofers in South Croydon, Waddon and Broad Green: start with the build date

Builders in each decade worked to their own habits, with their own materials and budgets. A slate villa from the 1850s, roofed with no felt at all, behaves nothing like a 1930s council house roofed in clay plain tiles that was re-covered in concrete forty years later. So the build date tells a roofer more than the postcode does.

<img src="/images/roof-repairs-croydon-loc-south-croydon-slate-roofs-tree-lined-road-w800.webp" alt="Tall white stucco-fronted Victorian houses on a tree-lined London street, with a red bus in the distance" width="800" height="534" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

It also tells you whether a repair or a replacement is the sensible spend. On the oldest houses a few new slates and some lead often keep the roof going for years more. On a roof that was already re-covered cheaply in the 1970s, it usually makes more sense to replace it. Our [South Croydon roofing page](/south-croydon) covers the neighbourhood side, and our [roof repair team in Croydon](/) handles the full range, from a single slipped slate to a full strip and re-cover.

The geography plays a part too. South Croydon sits on chalk hillsides either side of the gravelly Wandle valley, Waddon is down in that valley, and Broad Green to the north has a band of London Clay running between Mitcham Road and London Road. Clay shrinks in a dry summer and swells again in winter. That matters more to the walls than the roof, but if a crack runs up through the brickwork into a chimney stack, new flashings won't hold until someone has found the cause.

## 1830s to 1860s: Brighton Road's first terraces and The Waldrons villas

Building between the Brighton Road and the railway started in the 1830s, with early Victorian terraces mixed in with workshops and yards. A little later, from the 1850s, a private estate south of the town centre was laid out as The Waldrons, a crescent of large Italianate villas in London stock brick with stucco detailing, broad eaves and slate roofs. Big houses on Bramley Hill followed from the 1860s.

<img src="/images/blog/victorian-stone-lodge-slate-roof-waddon-south-croydon.webp" alt="Victorian stone lodge with a grey slate roof, a tall central chimney and an arched porch, set among trees" width="800" height="450" loading="lazy" />

Most of these roofs are natural slate on fairly shallow pitches, hipped or with wide overhanging eaves, and the chimney stacks are big. The council's appraisal for The Waldrons lists prominent stacks and slate among the features that define the area. Around St Peter's Church, designed by Sir George Gilbert Scott and built between 1849 and 1851, some of the villas on St Peter's Road and Blunt Road date from the 1850s and 1860s too.

### What tends to fail

On a slate roof this old, the slates themselves are usually fine. The nails holding them rust through, slates start sliding out one at a time, and the usual fix is a copper tingle. Once slipped slates turn up all over the roof, the nails have reached the end of their life everywhere and patching won't keep up. The other weak spot is the lead: big stacks have big flashings and back gutters, and on houses that age they have often been patched with cement or bitumen rather than renewed. Wide eaves bring a third problem, because the timber brackets and soffit boards under them rot once the gutter above overflows a few winters running.

## 1868 to 1890: Birdhurst Rise, St Peter's Road and the gabled villas

East of the railway, the Birdhurst Hill estate was broken up and sold as building plots in the early 1880s, and Birdhurst Road and Birdhurst Rise filled with detached and semi-detached houses between about 1868 and 1890. Fashions had changed by then. The council's description of the area picks out yellow stock brick with contrasting bands, red-brick villas with rectangular turrets, terracotta details and, on the roofs, bespoke timber bargeboards and finials along with corbelled eaves and chimneys.

<img src="/images/blog/red-brick-victorian-gables-slate-south-croydon.webp" alt="Row of steep red brick Victorian gables with dark slate roofs and arched windows under a pale sky" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

A steep gabled roof sheds water well, so the main slopes are rarely the worry here. The problems are at the edges and on top. Bargeboards are carved timber sitting fully exposed to the weather, and when the paint goes they split along the grain. Finials rot at the base and let water into the apex. A turret has a small roof with a lot of joints in it, often in lead or zinc, and rarely gets checked because nobody can see it from the garden. St Peter's Road has the same family of details, with decorative ridge tiles and ornate bargeboards on roofs of clay tile or natural slate, and the council notes that some have already been swapped for concrete tiles.

A lot of these houses are now flats. The council's own character study says many of South Croydon's big Victorian and Edwardian houses have been converted, which means a single roof can belong to three or four leaseholders. Before any work is agreed, someone has to work out who pays, and we wrote about that in [our guide to roof repairs in converted flats](/blog/who-pays-roof-repairs-croydon-converted-flats).

## 1900 to 1914: Broad Green's terraces and the flats over London Road shops

Broad Green takes its name from a small triangle of green where London Road, Hathaway Road and Kidderminster Road now meet. The Broadgreen Place estate beside it was sold for building in 1900. Hathaway Road and Nova Road were laid out that year and their houses went up in 1902, at the same time as the shopping parade with flats above at 206 to 272 London Road, named Royal Mansions for Edward VII's coronation. By 1914 Croydon and London had joined up through here. The council's character appraisal says the residential streets have hardly changed since the early 1900s.

<img src="/images/blog/london-stock-brick-terrace-snow-broad-green.webp" alt="End of a yellow stock brick terrace with an arched doorway and a chimney stack, beside snow-covered shrubs and a timber fence" width="900" height="608" loading="lazy" />

Hardly changed. That also means a lot of original roofs, or roofs on their first re-cover, now well over a century old. Expect slate or clay plain tile on the main roof, a chimney stack on each party wall shared with next door, and a two-storey rear wing with its own small roof meeting the main wall in a strip of lead or, more often by now, cement. That junction is where most leaks start on these streets. We've written more about it in [our post on outrigger roofs on Croydon terraces](/blog/outrigger-back-addition-roofs-croydon-victorian-terraces).

The flats over the shops are a different kind of job. The Royal Mansions frontage has decorative gables, rendered walls and a lot of joints where a gable meets the roof behind. Getting up there usually means scaffold over a busy pavement, which needs a licence from the council, and the flat owner is rarely the freeholder. Part of the parade, numbers 256 to 272, was demolished after fire damage in 2011, so the roofs along the parade aren't all the same age.

## 1920s and 1930s: the Waddon Estate and the Chapman houses of Croham Manor Road

Two very different kinds of interwar house sit within a mile of each other here. In the Wandle valley, Croydon started the Waddon Estate in the 1920s as council housing, and the council's appraisal says it still accounts for most of the homes in Waddon. London's first airport had opened on Purley Way, just to the west, in 1920. Up the hill, on Croham Manor Road and Hurst Way, a builder the council's records call Mr Chapman put up a group of individually designed mock Tudor houses in the late 1920s and early 1930s, and lived at number 56 himself.

<img src="/images/blog/timber-framed-house-steep-clay-tile-roof-croham.webp" alt="Black and white timber-framed house with a steep brown tiled roof and two tall brick chimneys, beside a lawn under blue sky" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

The Chapman roofs are fussy, in a good way. The appraisal lists steep handmade clay tiles, tile hanging on the walls, gablets, catslide roofs, dormers, timber bargeboards and big brick stacks. Handmade clay tiles can last a very long time, but the nails and the timber nibs that hold them up don't, and every dormer cheek and catslide valley is another joint to keep watertight. Finding replacement tiles that match is half the job, which is why salvaged handmade tiles are worth keeping when anything comes off.

The estate houses are plainer and quicker to work on. Council houses of that period were mostly brick pairs and short terraces under simple pitched or hipped roofs. On estates like this many houses were bought by their tenants and re-roofed at different times by different owners, so a terrace of four can have three types of tile on it. Where an old roof meets a newer one, the join along the party wall is a common leak, along with tired mortar on the ridges and verges and slipped concrete tiles after a windy night. Where the council still owns the freehold of a block, leaseholders usually need to go through the council's repairs process rather than book a roofer themselves.

## Do you need permission? South Croydon, Waddon and Broad Green rules compared

The short answer for most houses here is no. On an unlisted house, repairs that use matching materials don't need planning permission anywhere. The areas below are where you need to check before changing a roof, and the rules work differently in each.

<img src="/images/blog/london-garden-path-victorian-houses-conservation-croydon.webp" alt="Tree-lined garden path with a long row of benches leading towards red brick Victorian mansion blocks" width="800" height="533" loading="lazy" />

### The Waldrons

The Waldrons conservation area, designated in May 1973, sits inside Waddon ward between Duppas Hill, Haling Park and the A232 flyover. An Article 4 direction has applied to numbers 5 to 23 since 21 January 2016, and it is often misread. It removes the right to lay hard surfaces and to put up or alter gates, fences and walls without permission. It says nothing about roofs. The conservation area status still matters, though: the council's appraisal says roof extensions and dormers visible from the road are unlikely to be acceptable, treats non-traditional roofing materials as a threat, and says solar panels on slopes the road can see are not appropriate. The council's <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning/planning-policy/conservation-and-heritage/conservation-areas-directory/waldrons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">conservation area page for The Waldrons</a> links to the full appraisal.

### Croham Manor Road

Croham Manor Road conservation area was designated in 2008 and covers the 18 Chapman houses. It has no Article 4 direction, but the same cautions apply about solar panels visible from the road, and changing handmade clay for anything else on a front slope would be a hard sell.

### Local heritage areas

Local heritage areas cover London Road (Broad Green), St Peter's Road, Birdhurst Road and Birdhurst Rise, and South End with Ye Market. These add no consent requirements of their own and leave permitted development rights alone. They only count when an application is made. The <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning-policy/conservation-and-heritage/local-heritage-areas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Croydon Council guide to local heritage areas</a> explains how they're used.

### Everywhere else

Everywhere else, including the whole Waddon Estate, ordinary permitted development applies. One more Article 4 direction does cover the whole borough. Since 28 January 2020 it has required planning permission to turn a house into a small house in multiple occupation, but it has nothing to do with roofs. The council keeps all of them on its <a href="https://www.croydon.gov.uk/planning-and-regeneration/planning-policy/article-4-directions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Article 4 directions page</a>.
