A roof problem does not stop a Croydon house from selling. It changes how much it sells for, how long it takes and how much stress sits in the middle, and the size of all three depends on a decision most sellers make by accident. You can fix the roof before the board goes up. You can price the house to reflect it and say so. Or you can leave it, let the buyer's surveyor find it and negotiate from there. Croydon is the most populous borough in London, with around 390,800 people at the last census and a housing stock that is largely Victorian, Edwardian and interwar, so there are a great many roofs in the borough between fifty and a hundred and thirty years old going through this exact conversation every month. This is how the three routes actually play out, what the law makes you say, and which repairs are worth paying for before you list.

## Why the roof always comes up in a Croydon sale

Sellers tend to file the roof under background detail. Buyers, surveyors and lenders do not. It is the most expensive single element of a house to replace and the hardest for a buyer to judge from the pavement.

<img src="/images/roof-repairs-croydon-site-london-residential-street-parked-cars-croydon-w1200.webp" alt="A residential Croydon street of brick houses with cars parked along the kerb" width="900" height="600" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

The age of the borough's housing does most of the work here. A bay-fronted Edwardian terrace in Thornton Heath has a roof that is well past its first century. A 1930s semi in Shirley or Purley is approaching ninety. The concrete tiles that replaced many original roofs in the 1960s and 70s are themselves fifty or sixty years old. Any surveyor walking up to one of those houses expects to find something, and usually does. Their report will then put a figure on it, or more often tell the buyer to get a roofer's quote, and the buyer will come back to you with a number that is almost always higher than what the work would actually cost. If you want to know what a repair actually runs to before that happens, our guide to [roof repair costs in Croydon](/roof-repair-cost) gives realistic ranges by job.

That is the heart of it. Whoever gets the first quote sets the terms. If it is you, the figure is real. If it is the buyer, the figure is a negotiating position.

## What the buyer's survey will say about it

Almost every buyer with a mortgage gets a survey, and in Croydon's price bracket most choose a RICS Home Survey at Level 2 or Level 3. It is worth understanding what each one does with a roof, because that decides how visible the problem becomes.

<img src="/images/blog/surveyor-clipboard-house-exterior-croydon-sale.webp" alt="A surveyor with a clipboard inspecting the outside of a house before a sale" width="900" height="601" loading="lazy" />

The <a href="https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/building-surveying-standards/home-surveys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">RICS home survey standard</a> sets out three levels. Level 1 is a basic condition check. Level 2, the most common on an ordinary house, inspects the roof from ground level, from a ladder at the eaves where it is safe, and from inside the loft, and then gives each element a condition rating from 1 to 3. Level 3, the full building survey, does the same but writes up the cause of each defect and what putting it right involves. On an older Croydon roof the coverings, the chimneys and the flat roofs over extensions are the elements most likely to come back as a rating 3, which means serious or urgent, or a rating 2 with a recommendation to get a roofer to look.

Two things follow. First, the surveyor is not going on the roof, so a good deal of what they report is inference: moss and slipped tiles seen from the ground, staining seen in the loft, a felt roof judged from a bedroom window. That inference is cautious by design, and cautious reads as expensive. Second, whatever they write goes to the buyer and, in a shortened form, to the lender. The report is the moment the roof stops being your private knowledge and becomes everyone's problem.

## Fix it, price it or leave it: the three routes compared

There is no single right answer. There is a wrong one, though, and it is the route most sellers drift into.

<img src="/images/blog/couple-weighing-roof-repair-costs-croydon-sale.webp" alt="A couple at a kitchen table weighing up repair costs before selling their house" width="900" height="645" loading="lazy" />

Fixing first means paying for the repair yourself, before the photographs are taken. The advantage is control. You choose the roofer, you get a real quote, the work is done at a fair price and the surveyor finds a sound roof with a recent invoice and a guarantee behind it. The disadvantage is cash up front at a point when you are also paying for solicitors and removals. It suits any defect that is small, visible and clearly diagnosable.

Pricing it in means listing the house at a figure that reflects the roof and telling the agent to say so. Honest buyers like this. It removes the ambush from the survey, and a roof that has already been priced in cannot be used to chip the price a second time. It suits a bigger job, a re-roof or a whole flat roof, where the cost is too high to absorb but too obvious to hide. The risk is that buyers value a known problem more harshly than it deserves, so you should get a written quote yourself and hand it over with the listing.

Leaving it means saying nothing, letting the survey find it and dealing with the renegotiation when it comes. This is where sellers lose the most money. The buyer's figure is inflated, their solicitor gets involved, the lender may impose a retention, and the whole thing lands in week eight of a chain when everyone is tired and any delay risks the sale. A repair that would have cost you £600 in March becomes a £3,000 price reduction in June, and you had no say in it.

## What you must disclose, and what happens if you don't

British property law still works on the principle that the buyer finds out for themselves, but that principle has narrowed a great deal, and roof problems sit right on the line.

<img src="/images/blog/signing-property-information-form-croydon.webp" alt="A seller signing the property information form for a house sale" width="900" height="601" loading="lazy" />

Your solicitor will ask you to complete the <a href="https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/property/ta6-6th-edition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TA6 Property Information Form</a>, which is now in its sixth edition. It asks about building works, about guarantees and warranties on the property, about buildings insurance claims and about disputes with neighbours, among other things. There is no box that says "does the roof leak", but a leak you have had repaired is building work, a claim you made after a storm is an insurance claim, and a row with next door over the shared valley is a dispute. Answer any of those untruthfully and the buyer has a misrepresentation claim they can bring after completion, when the leak reappears and they find the old invoice in a drawer.

The estate agent has a separate duty. Agents are bound by consumer protection law not to mislead buyers by what they say or by what they leave out, so once you have told the agent about the roof they cannot un-know it. Sellers sometimes read this as a reason not to tell the agent. It is the reverse. An agent who knows can present the roof properly and price it in, which protects you. An agent who finds out from the survey has been made to look foolish, and the buyer now wonders what else you kept quiet.

## Retentions, chains and the cost of delay

The most damaging thing a roof does to a sale is not the repair cost. It is the time.

<img src="/images/blog/estate-agent-window-listings-croydon.webp" alt="Property listings in an estate agent's window" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

If the lender's valuer flags the roof, the lender can impose a retention, holding back part of the mortgage until the work is done and signed off. The buyer then has a hole in their funding. They either find the money themselves, which most first-time buyers pushing out to Croydon from inner London cannot, or they ask you to drop the price by the retention amount, or the work has to be done before completion by someone. Every one of those options takes weeks, and in a chain weeks are what kill sales.

There is a practical Croydon wrinkle as well. Roof work on a terrace or a house on a controlled parking street often needs scaffolding on the pavement, which means a council licence and possibly a parking suspension, and those run to their own timetable. We set that out in our piece on [scaffolding and access costs in a Croydon roof quote](/blog/scaffolding-access-croydon-roof-quotes). If a repair has to happen mid-sale, that lead time is added to a chain that was already fragile. Doing it before you list removes the whole problem. The government's guide to <a href="https://www.gov.uk/buy-sell-your-home" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">buying and selling your home</a> sets out how many stages sit between an offer and completion, and a roof repair dropped into the middle of them pushes every one back.

## Which repairs pay for themselves before listing

Not every roof job is worth doing before a sale. The test is simple: will the repair cost less than the price reduction the buyer would otherwise demand, and does it remove the item from the survey entirely?

<img src="/images/blog/roofer-cherry-picker-repair-before-sale-croydon.webp" alt="A roofer on a cherry picker repairing a red brick house before it goes on the market" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

The ones that nearly always pass that test are small and visible. Slipped, cracked or missing tiles, which a surveyor spots from the pavement and which cost a morning to put right. Cracked or missing ridge and verge mortar, which reads as neglect. A chimney flashing that has lifted, since a chimney with a visible lead problem gets a rating 3 almost automatically. A leaking gutter joint or a sagging run, because staining down the brickwork is the first thing a viewer sees. And any ceiling stain from a leak that has already been fixed, which must be redecorated, because a buyer seeing a brown patch assumes the leak is live no matter what you tell them.

The ones that usually fail the test are the big ones. A full re-roof on a house that is otherwise fine rarely returns its cost in the sale price, because buyers do not pay a premium for a new roof, they merely stop deducting for an old one. The exception is a roof that really is at the end, where the survey will trigger a retention and no lender will advance the full loan. There the question is not whether to spend but whether to spend it as a repair or a reduction, and we worked through how to tell a tired roof from a finished one in [repair or replace: is your Croydon roof actually finished?](/blog/repair-or-replace-croydon-roof). Somewhere between those sits the felt flat roof on the rear extension, which is expensive enough to hurt and visible enough to be found, and is usually best priced in with a quote attached.

## Paperwork that makes a roof a selling point

Once the work is done, the paper is worth almost as much as the repair. A surveyor who finds a recent invoice, a written guarantee and photographs of the finished work will note the roof as recently repaired rather than as a defect, and the buyer's solicitor will stop asking questions.

<img src="/images/blog/house-keys-completion-day-croydon.webp" alt="House keys being handed over on completion day" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

So ask your roofer for the things a buyer will want to see. A dated invoice that describes the work in plain terms, not just "roof repairs". A written workmanship guarantee, ideally transferable to the new owner, and the manufacturer's paperwork on any materials that carry their own warranty. Photographs from the scaffold or the ladder of the work as completed, since a buyer cannot get up there either. And for a re-roof, the building control completion certificate, because replacing more than a quarter of a roof covering is notifiable work and a missing certificate is a question the buyer's solicitor will raise. Put all of it in the pack you give the agent and the solicitor on day one.

Handled this way, the roof stops being the thing that might sink the sale and becomes one of the few parts of an old Croydon house the buyer does not have to worry about. That is a better position than most sellers ever get to, and it costs a phone call in the spring rather than an argument in the summer.
